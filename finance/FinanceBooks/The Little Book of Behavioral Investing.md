"The Little Book of Behavioral Investing" by James Montier, here are the most important points and "behavioral defenses" organized by each chapter:																									
																									
I. The Core Psychological Trap																									
																									
Introduction: This Is a Book About You																									
																									
The market isn't the problem; your biological hard-wiring is. Investing is the one field where our natural instincts (empathy, quick reactions, social bonding) actually work against us.																									
																									
Chapter 1: In the Heat of the Moment																									
																									
When the market crashes or surges, your "emotional brain" (System X) hijacks your "logical brain" (System C).																									
																									
Key Point: You cannot make rational decisions under high stress. You must create rules while you are calm to follow when things get chaotic.																									
																									
Chapter 2: Who’s Afraid of the Big Bad Market?																									
																									
Loss aversion is the dominant human trait. The pain of a loss is twice as potent as the joy of a gain.																									
																									
Key Point: This fear causes people to "sell at the bottom" just to stop the psychological pain.																									
																									
Chapter 3: Always Look on the Bright Side of Life																									
																									
Humans are naturally optimistic, which leads to Overconfidence Bias. We overestimate our ability to pick winners and underestimate risks.																									
II. The Noise and the Experts																									
																									
Chapter 4: Why Does Anyone Listen to These Guys?																									
																									
Wall Street analysts have a massive "optimism bias." Their incentives are aligned with selling stocks, not necessarily being right.																									
																									
Chapter 5: The Folly of Forecasting																									
																									
Economic and market forecasts are almost always wrong. The world is too complex for linear predictions.																									
																									
Key Point: Don't build an investment strategy based on a "prediction" of where the S&P 500 will be in 12 months.																									
																									
Chapter 6: Information Overload																									
																									
More information does not equal more accuracy; it only equals more confidence.																									
																									
Key Point: Focus on the few "vital" facts of a business rather than the "trivial many."																									
																									
Chapter 7: Turn off That Bubblevision!																									
																									
Financial media (CNBC, etc.) creates a sense of urgency that forces you into the "System X" emotional state.																									
																									
Chapter 8: See No Evil, Hear No Evil																									
																									
Confirmation Bias: We ignore information that proves our stocks are bad. Montier suggests actively seeking out the "Bear Case" for every stock you own.																									
III. Strategic Pitfalls																									
																									
Chapter 9: In the Land of the Perma-Bear and the Perma-Bull																									
																									
Avoid ideology. Being "always bullish" or "always bearish" is a cognitive shortcut that ignores reality.																									
																									
Chapter 10: The Siren Song of Stories																									
																									
We love a good "story" stock (e.g., "This company is the next Tesla!").																									
																									
Key Point: Stories appeal to emotions. Stick to the cold, hard numbers of valuation instead.																									
																									
Chapter 11: This Time Is Different																									
																									
These are the four most expensive words in investing. Every bubble feels "new," but human psychology remains the same.																									
IV. Managing the Portfolio																									
																									
Chapter 12: Right for the Wrong Reason, or Wrong for the Right Reason																									
																									
Process vs. Outcome: You can be "lucky" and make money on a bad bet. This is dangerous because it reinforces bad habits.																									
																									
Key Point: Judge yourself by your process, not your short-term profit.																									
																									
Chapter 13: The Perils of ADHD Investing																									
																									
High portfolio turnover (over-trading) is a result of the "Action Bias"—the feeling that we must do something when the market moves.																									
																									
Chapter 14: Inside the Mind of a Lemming																									
																									
Herd Behavior: Following the crowd is socially safe but financially ruinous. Being a contrarian is psychologically painful but necessary for high returns.																									
																									
Chapter 15: You Gotta Know When to Fold Them																									
																									
Investors struggle to admit they are wrong.																									
																									
Key Point: If the original reason you bought a stock is no longer true, sell it immediately. Don't wait to "break even."																									
V. The Solution																									
																									
Chapter 16: Process, Process, Process																									
																									
The only way to beat your own brain is to have a Checklist. A rigorous, repeatable process removes the "heat of the moment" from the equation.																									
																									
Conclusion: The Road to Hell Is Paved with Good Intentions																									
																									
Knowing your biases isn't enough; you must have the discipline to apply the defenses.
