Quantum computing, explained with a spinning coin

No physics background needed. Play with the coin, then work through the short labs below. By the end you'll know what a qubit is, why people are excited about it, and which claims are hype.

Start from the beginning

The coin is spinning. Until you look, it has no single answer: it is a blend of 0 and 1.

Start with a light switch

Every phone and laptop you've used is built from billions of tiny switches called transistors. Each one is either off (0) or on (1). A single switch holds one bit, the smallest unit of information. Line up eight and you can write any number from 0 to 255. Line up billions and you can store a photo, a song, or this page.

Here is the limit that matters for what follows: a row of switches is in exactly one arrangement at any moment. To explore many possibilities, an ordinary computer has to try them one after another, or use many more switches side by side.

Try it: eight switches, one number

The little numbers above each switch are its worth. Add up the worths of the switches that are 1 to get the number. Notice that the row only ever shows one pattern at a time.

A qubit is like a spinning coin

A coin lying on a table is heads or tails, just like a bit. But spin it, and while it blurs round on its edge it isn't cleanly either. A qubit (quantum bit) is the quantum version of that spinning coin. Until you look, it sits in a blend of 0 and 1 called a superposition. When you look, which scientists call measuring, you get one definite answer.

The analogy

Spin a coin and ask "heads or tails?" while it's spinning. There's no honest single answer yet. Slap it flat and you get one. Measuring a qubit works the same way: it forces a single result, and the result is random, with odds set by how the spin was prepared.

How is the blend described? A qubit carries two numbers called amplitudes, one for 0 and one for 1. Square an amplitude and you get the chance of seeing that result. A balanced spin gives 50/50. A lopsided spin favors one side. Use the dial below to lopside it yourself.

Each measurement is a fresh random draw, so a few measurements look noisy. Do a few hundred and the results settle toward the prediction.

Where the coin analogy stops. A real spinning coin is secretly heads-up or tails-up at every instant, we just can't see it in the blur. A qubit is genuinely undecided until measured. Also, amplitudes can be negative (even complex numbers), which a coin's odds can't be. That detail is what powers the chapter on interference.

Why a few qubits can juggle so much

This is where quantum computing gets its reputation. n ordinary bits can be in one of 2n patterns, but only one at a time. n qubits keep a separate amplitude for every one of those 2n patterns at the same moment. Every qubit you add doubles the amount of information the machine is juggling.

The analogy

A row of ordinary switches is a single hiker standing on one spot of a trail map. A register of qubits is a fog spread over the whole map, thicker in some places and thinner in others, with every spot holding its own amount of fog at once.

It's not a free lunch. When you measure, the fog collapses to a single spot. You can't read out all those amplitudes. Quantum algorithms are clever ways of shaping the fog so that the spot you land on is probably the useful one.

Steering a qubit with gates

Ordinary computers transform bits with logic gates such as AND, OR and NOT. Quantum computers have gates too, but they rotate the qubit's blend instead of just flipping a switch. To picture one qubit, imagine a globe called the Bloch sphere. The north pole is a certain 0, the south pole is a certain 1, and every other point is some blend. A gate turns the arrow that points at the qubit's state.

Four gates to play with: X flips 0 and 1 (the quantum NOT). H (Hadamard) turns a certain 0 or 1 into a perfect 50/50 blend. Z flips the sign of the amplitude for 1, which changes nothing you can see on its own but matters a lot in combination. S is a quarter-turn version of Z.

Did the first experiment surprise you? Two Hadamards in a row bring back a certain 0. Nothing random about it. Something subtler than shuffling is going on, and the next chapter explains it.

The real trick: making wrong answers cancel

Amplitudes behave like waves. When two waves meet they can add up (a peak meets a peak) or cancel out (a peak meets a valley). That's interference, and it's the engine of quantum computing. It also explains the H-then-H result: the two paths that lead to "1" cancel each other, and the two paths that lead to "0" add up.

The analogy

Noise-cancelling headphones listen to the racket around you and play the exact opposite wave. Noise plus anti-noise gives silence. Or think of two ripples on a pond: where they line up the water jumps higher, and where they oppose each other it stays flat.

Try it: slide one wave against the other

A quantum algorithm is a choreography. First spread the qubits across all the possible answers. Then apply gates so that the paths leading to wrong answers cancel and the paths leading to the right answer reinforce each other. When you finally measure, the right answer is the likely one.

Here is a tiny real example. Sixteen doors, one hides the prize. You can't peek, but you have an "oracle" that can recognize the winning door when asked, in a quantum-friendly way. Each round has two steps, and you control them.

What you just did is the core of Grover's search algorithm, named after its inventor. Step 1 flips the sign of the right door's wave. Step 2 reflects every bar around the average, which makes the flipped bar grow and the others shrink. For N doors you need about √N rounds instead of checking roughly N/2 doors one by one. It's a real speedup, but a moderate one.

Entanglement: a link between qubits

Two qubits can be prepared so that their results are tied together, even though neither has a fixed value on its own. This is entanglement.

The analogy

Two "magic" coins are handed to two friends far apart. Each coin on its own looks like a fair coin: heads half the time, tails half the time. But when the friends compare notes, the coins always agree.

A pair of gloves split between two boxes would also match, but there the answer was settled before anyone opened a box. Experiments on entangled particles show that nothing like that is happening: the outcome genuinely wasn't fixed in advance.

Try it: independent coins versus an entangled pair

Results so far

No messages travel this way. Look at coin A alone and you only ever see random 0s and 1s. The match appears only when the two friends compare results over an ordinary channel, like a phone call. So entanglement cannot send information faster than light.

Why does it matter? Entanglement lets the amplitudes of many qubits become linked into patterns that no set of independent coins could produce. That linking is part of what lets interference act on a whole group of qubits at once, and it's a big reason ordinary computers struggle to imitate quantum ones.

Why they're so hard to build

Superposition is delicate. A stray vibration, a bit of heat or a wandering radio wave can act as an accidental measurement, which pushes the qubit to a plain 0 or 1 and ruins the calculation. This loss is called decoherence.

Real qubits are physical things: tiny superconducting circuits chilled to a hair above absolute zero, single charged atoms held in place by lasers, individual particles of light, and more. Engineers go to extreme lengths (vacuum chambers, deep refrigeration, shielding) to keep the outside world from "looking".

The analogy

A spinning coin on a bumpy table. Every bump steals a little spin until the coin falls over, however well you launched it. The longer your calculation, the more bumps it has to survive.

Qubits also make small mistakes every time a gate acts on them. The cure is quantum error correction: spread one reliable "logical" qubit over many physical qubits that keep checking each other for slips, without ever looking at the answer itself. It works in principle and has been demonstrated at small scale, but it's expensive. A useful machine may need hundreds or thousands of physical qubits for each logical one.

What they're actually for

A quantum computer isn't a faster laptop. It's a different kind of machine that is dramatically better at a few specific jobs and no better at most others.

Simulating nature

Molecules and materials obey quantum rules, which makes them brutally hard for ordinary computers to simulate. A quantum computer speaks the same language. Hoped-for payoffs include better batteries, fertilizers, drugs and catalysts.

Breaking certain locks

Shor's algorithm (named after its inventor) can factor huge numbers far faster than any known ordinary method. That would threaten some of today's public-key encryption. Quantum-resistant replacements are already being standardized.

Searching and some optimizing

Grover-style methods give a square-root speedup for unstructured search, like the doors game. Helpful in some settings, but not magic.

Everyday computing

Email, video, games, spreadsheets, web pages. For these, your laptop is cheaper, faster and simpler, and a quantum computer offers no advantage.

Keep these five ideas

  • Qubit: like a spinning coin, a blend of 0 and 1 until measured.
  • Scale: n qubits carry 2n amplitudes at once, but you only ever read out one result.
  • Interference: amplitudes act like waves. Algorithms make wrong answers cancel and right answers add up.
  • Entanglement: linked qubits share patterns that independent ones can't, without sending any message.
  • Reality check: qubits are fragile, today's machines are small, and quantum computers help with specific problems, not everything.

Quick glossary

Bit

A switch that is 0 or 1. The building block of ordinary computers.

Qubit

A quantum bit: any physical system that can be prepared in a blend of two states, used as 0 and 1.

Superposition

The blend of 0 and 1 a qubit is in before it is measured.

Amplitude

A number attached to each possible result. Squaring it gives the chance of seeing that result. Amplitudes can be negative, which is what allows cancellation.

Measurement

Looking at a qubit. It gives one definite 0 or 1 and leaves the qubit in that state.

Gate

An operation that changes a qubit's state, such as X, H, Z or S above.

Interference

Amplitudes adding up or cancelling, like overlapping waves.

Entanglement

A link between qubits so their results are correlated even though neither is fixed on its own.

Decoherence

Loss of a qubit's delicate blend because the environment disturbs it.

Error correction

Using many physical qubits to protect one logical qubit from errors.

Analogies simplify. The spinning coin, the fog and the gloves each get something right and something wrong, and the "Where the analogy stops" notes say where. To go deeper, look for university-level introductions to qubits, the Bloch sphere, and Grover's and Shor's algorithms.
