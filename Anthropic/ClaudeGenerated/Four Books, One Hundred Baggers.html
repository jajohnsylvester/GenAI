100 Baggers

Christopher W. Mayer · 365 US stocks that returned 100x, 1962–2014

Hundred-fold winners share a pattern. Earnings grow for a long time, the market pays a higher multiple for them, and the owner never sells the compounding.

  • Twin enginesEarnings growth and a rising P/E multiply together. One alone rarely reaches 100x.
  • Start smallMost winners began as small companies with a large market still ahead of them.
  • High returns on capital, low debtThe business funds its own growth. Look for ROE or ROCE well above 15%.
  • Owner-operators and a moatFounders with real stakes, and an advantage that is hard to copy.
  • TimeMany 100-baggers took twenty years or more. The coffee can idea: buy, then leave it alone.
In IndiaStart with ₹500–5,000 crore companies in categories where Indian per-capita use is still low. Prefer promoter-led firms with steady ROCE, and check how much of the market they can still capture.

One Up on Wall Street

Peter Lynch · the ten-bagger and the two-minute story

You already see great businesses in daily life, before analysts do. Research them, sort them into a type, and pay a fair price for the growth.

  • Invest in what you knowThe shop, product or software your work or family uses. Then confirm the numbers.
  • Know which of six types it isSlow growers, stalwarts, fast growers, cyclicals, turnarounds, asset plays. Each has its own rules for buying and selling.
  • PEG at or below 1P/E divided by the earnings growth rate. A fair price for growth sits near 1, and above 2 is expensive.
  • Two-minute storyIf you cannot explain why it will grow in two minutes, you do not own it, you only hold a ticker.
  • Avoid hot stocks and "diworsification"Be wary of fashionable sectors and of firms that buy unrelated businesses.
In IndiaNotice the brand sold in every kirana, the bank your office uses, the supplier your employer depends on. Look for fast growers near 20–25% earnings growth, and treat very high rates with suspicion.

Coffee Can Investing

Saurabh Mukherjea with Pranab Uniyal and Rakshit Ranjan · built on Indian data

Pick a few companies with a long, boring record of high returns and steady growth, put them in the coffee can, and do not open it for ten years.

  • Ten-year record, every yearRevenue growth of at least 10% and ROCE of at least 15%, in each of ten years. A good average is not enough.
  • Skip the lumpy and the fadingCyclical swings and slow declines fail the test even when a single year looks great.
  • Check the accountsProfit should turn into cash, capital should be allocated sensibly, and the reported numbers should be clean.
  • Buy and do not tradeLow turnover is part of the method. Selling winners is the usual way investors give up the return.
In IndiaPull ten years of sales and ROCE from a screener, drop banks and other lenders from this particular test, then read cash flow and promoter pledging before you buy.

Multibaggers

Prasenjit Paul · the SQGLP checklist for Indian small caps

Multibaggers cluster in small companies run by people you can trust, growing for years, at a price that still leaves room. Score each trait, and walk away if one is missing.

  • S · Small sizeLow market value leaves room to grow several-fold.
  • Q · QualityQuality of the business and, above all, of the management and its governance.
  • G · GrowthEarnings that can compound for many years, with a clear reason why.
  • L · LongevityA franchise that will still be needed in twenty years.
  • P · PriceReasonable against the growth you expect. A great business bought dear returns less.
In IndiaSmall and micro caps carry governance risk, thin trading and sudden regulatory attention. Spend the most time on Q, and size the position so a miss does not hurt.

Process

From first idea to a decade of holding

The order matters here. Each step uses a different book, and a company that fails an early step does not reach the next.

  1. Lynch

    Notice

    Start from what you meet in daily life and at work. A brand with queues, a supplier everyone uses, a service that keeps winning customers. Write the two-minute story.

  2. Coffee Can · Mayer

    Screen the numbers

    Ten years of revenue growth at 10% or better, ROCE at 15% or better, low debt, a market value that still leaves room to grow.

  3. Paul · Mayer

    Test the people and the moat

    Promoter track record, pledged shares, related-party dealings, auditor changes. Name the advantage and say what could erase it.

  4. Lynch · Paul

    Check the price

    PEG near or below 1, and a P/E that does not already assume everything goes right. Expect to wait for a fair price more than once.

  5. Mayer · Coffee Can

    Hold and review

    Reread the annual report each year. Sell when the story or the accounts break, not because the price doubled. Keep position sizes small enough to stay calm.

Indian red flags, checked before step 4
Promoter pledgingShares pledged for loans can be sold in a falling market, and the price spirals.
Profit without cashProfit that never shows up as operating cash flow, and receivables that keep growing.
Related-party dealsLarge dealings with companies the promoters own, and loans to group firms.
Auditor exitsResignations, qualified opinions or frequent changes without a clear reason.
Thin tradingYou may not be able to sell a small-cap position at the price you see.
Capital raised oftenRepeated dilution, or money raised for projects that never reach the earnings line.
